Totality is a thin ribbon

The Moon's shadow falls on Earth often. The part of it that blots the Sun out completely is a band a few hundred kilometres across, and it lands on any one place rarely. Here are the 67 tabulated paths of this century on a globe you can spin, drawn at NASA's real width rather than one thickened to be seen — thin almost everywhere, flaring only where the shadow arrives at sunrise and leaves at sunset. Play the century and they light in date order, ending on 12 August 2026: over Greenland, clipping Iceland, out over Spain at sunset.

Europe burns in bad years

Forty-five fire seasons in the five Mediterranean countries EFFIS has recorded without a break since 1980, stacked one year at a time. The shape is not a slope, it is a skyline: ordinary summers have been getting smaller since the 1980s, and the catastrophes have not moved — 1985 and 2017 still stand almost level. Pull a country out of the stack and its own bad years step forward.

The sky is full of clocks

Pulsars are the most precise natural clocks in the universe — dead neutron stars sweeping a beam past Earth on a fixed period. Here is every one we've measured, drawn on the galactic sky and blinking at its real spin: millisecond pulsars flicker almost too fast to follow, second-long ones pulse slow and heavy. Drag to keep only the fastest and the sky thins to the millisecond swarms.

A century of storms hitting Earth

The Sun's spots are one thing; the disturbance they hurl at us is another. The planetary Kp index has logged Earth's geomagnetic weather every three hours since 1932. Feel it through a compass needle: quiet years barely stir it, the eleven-year cycle breathes in the baseline, and the great storms — the 1989 blackout, the 2003 Halloween storms, 2024's Gannon event — swing it to the pin and flood the sky green.

The Loop

Every stage of every Tour de France since 1903, drawn as one route object. Press play and the race writes itself across France — the early point-to-point giants reaching clear across the country, the hexagon settling in through the middle century, the modern loops that dart abroad for a Grand Départ. A hundred and twenty-three editions, overlaid, become the shape of the race itself.

Seventy-five years of American tornadoes

Every tornado NOAA logged since 1950 — more than 73,000 of them, each drawn from touchdown to lift-off and colored by strength. Press play and three-quarters of a century accretes: the haze of weak twisters, the deep-red spokes of the violent few, and the shape of Tornado Alley emerging from the count.

Ten thousand years of eruptions

The planet is never quiet. Put every eruption the Smithsonian has a record of on one map — colored and sized by explosivity — and press play, and the whole restless Earth lights up through deep time: the gentle lava fields, and the handful of cataclysms that darkened whole skies.

The Sun draws butterflies

Plot sunspots by the latitude they appear at and a pattern nobody designed comes out: every eleven years a cycle opens near 30°, marches to the equator, and dies there as the next one opens above it. A century and a half of it, rotation by rotation.

Rivers keep time

Six American rivers drawn as a single year each. The Willamette peaks in January, the Yellowstone in June when the snowpack goes. And the Colorado below Glen Canyon Dam barely has a year at all — we switched its seasons off in 1963.

The days nobody is born

Twenty-one years of American birth records on a calendar, with the weekend effect divided out. Christmas Day runs 41% below an ordinary day, the 13th of the month dips every time, and mid-September glows. Birth is scheduled now, and it shows.

The tide is not one thing

A year of tides at six American harbours, each day drawn as one petal on a 24-hour clock. Anchorage heaves eleven metres; Honolulu barely breathes. Same ocean, same moon — completely different signatures.

Every world we've ever found

Every confirmed planet beyond the Sun — 6,000-plus worlds on one turning field, each orbiting at its true relative speed, placed by how long its year is and colored by how we found it. The inner blur is our blind spot made visible.
